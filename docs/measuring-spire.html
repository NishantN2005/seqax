Measuring SPIRe

Putting a stopwatch to a speculative-decoding cost model that was only ever computed on paper.

Nishant N. · UC Irvine seqax fork · branch spire_reproducer H100 PCIe 80GB · JAX 0.6.0 updated 2026-09-19
Abstract

SPIRe (Neelam et al., MatX, arXiv:2504.06419) reports roughly 2× throughput over vanilla speculative decoding and 35% over a MagicDec-style baseline. Those figures come from an analytical roofline cost model, not from measurement. This work reproduces the paper's models from scratch — a 67M-parameter target and three drafts, all architectures and token budgets exactly as specified — and measures what the model predicts.

All three of the paper's draft models are now trained, and Table 1 is reproduced in full — nine cells across three methods and three context lengths, at the paper's own configuration. Acceptance lands within 2% for the dense baseline and 6–8% for the two sparse methods, with the ordering correct at every context length. The shape reproduces more closely than the level: MagicDec is the only method whose acceptance genuinely drifts with context length, and it drifts in the same direction and by nearly the same amount as published.

The cost model is then measured. It is optimistic by a strikingly flat 25% across 24 configurations, and every absolute throughput lands at 0.75–0.76 of prediction — but the paper’s claims are ratios between methods, and a common factor cancels from a ratio. Its headline “over 100% versus vanilla, over 35% versus MagicDec” measures on hardware as +104% and +33%. The comparative conclusions survive measurement; the absolute ones do not. Two things emerge that only measurement can show: vanilla speculative decoding is a net loss in this regime, and depth behaves asymmetrically — the dense draft has a sharp optimum it degrades badly past, while both sparse drafts are flat from k≈4 onward.

Finally the method’s central mechanism is measured, which the paper never does: the claim that a windowed draft gets cheaper relative to the target as context grows. Over a 5× context range the dense control holds flat while both sparse drafts fall, and SPIRe’s measured margin over vanilla grows from 1.99× to 2.50×. The paper’s headline +104% turns out to be a floor — quoted at the shortest context in its own grid, where the mechanism has barely engaged. The same sweep finds a trend running the other way that the paper’s context range is too short to see: SPIRe’s lead over MagicDec shrinks with context.

Along the way the reproduction recovers a sampling temperature the paper never states, shows that at greedy decoding the ranking of the three methods inverts, and extends the analysis to speculation depths k = 1…8 where the paper reports only k = 4. It also finds that the paper's cost model charges a sparse draft for a cache one speculation depth too small to actually run, and that the extra FLOPs term it adds for SPIRe changes nothing at any operating point it plots.

It also reports the corrections this reproduction had to make to itself — two findings retracted outright once the paper was read in full rather than reconstructed from its appendix, and one architecture rebuilt after three training runs against the wrong operator. Those are given equal weight here on purpose. Every wrong result looked healthy from inside the training loop, and the ones that survived did so because they were checked against something outside it.

The mechanism is clean. MagicDec’s draft is the target, so its distribution matches exactly and Leviathan’s min(1, p/q) accepts readily at any temperature. A distilled draft is trained to match distributions, so it changes least. An independently trained draft agrees on the argmax far more often than on the distribution — which greedy decoding rewards and sampling penalizes.

This is a confound in the paper’s headline comparison, and it is larger than the comparison. Greedy decoding compresses three methods into a 0.21-point band and reverses which of SPIRe and vanilla is better, on a hyperparameter the paper never records. Any τ reported without a stated temperature is under-determined by more than the effect being claimed.

4b

A finding this work retractedretracted

An earlier version of this report claimed that acceptance varies substantially with context length while the cost model assumes it constant. That claim was wrong on both halves, and the error is instructive.

The paper measures it. Table 1 reports τ at L = 256, 512 and 960 for all three methods. Vanilla varies 0.4% across that range, SPIRe 1.3%, MagicDec 5.3%. The constancy assumption is stated and supported with data, not smuggled in.

And the variation measured here was self-inflicted

SPIRe and MagicDec use a StreamingLLM mask with a window fixed at 64 tokens at every context length — the paper is explicit that the KV cache size is constant with respect to sequence length. This reproduction scaled the window as L/8, a parameter taken from a reconstruction of the appendix rather than from the paper. At L=512 the two coincide, which is exactly why that column reproduced and the others did not.

So the 14% spread reported earlier was a window sweep wearing a context-length label. Those measurements are withdrawn. They have since been retaken at a fixed window — that is the table in §2 — and the corrected version agrees with the paper: vanilla and SPIRe are flat in L to within 1.1%, MagicDec falls 6.4% across the range against the paper’s 5.6%. The real effect was smaller than the artifact that hid it, and in the same direction as the paper’s own data rather than against it.

What survives

The underlying observation is still real, just not a criticism of the paper: a sparse draft is sharply sensitive to a change in its window, and asymmetrically so.

5

Feedback memory: three runs against the wrong mechanism, then rebuiltcorrected

Three full training runs went into SPIRe’s feedback memory, and all three measured something the paper does not describe. The error was not at the margins — it was the wrong operation.

The paper’s §3.3 specifies zℓt = Attention(xℓt, m<t) — the memory replaces the keys and values, and is read from previous positions. This work implemented x = x + Σ w·bank: an additive injection at the same position. Everything that followed was downstream of that.
What each wrong conclusion actually followed from.
Concluded hereWhy it does not hold
Slot j at layer j is the residual stream, so the mechanism is a rescaletrue only when memory is consumed at the same position; real memory is read at later ones
One usable scalar for a 2-layer draftcapacity is attention over every past position’s memory, not six coefficients
A depth-causal mask is requiredprevents a cycle that never existed — mt is built from all of position t and read only after it
Including the target’s final layer is an information leakit is legitimate context, never used to predict its own position

What the specification actually says

Per-layer memory vectors, not shared: mit is a softmax-weighted sum over the draft’s nlayer+1 activations at position t, layer 0 being the embedding. The substitution is a direct assignment rather than a mix — for t ≤ S−k, m1t = y6t and m2t = y7t, the target’s layers 6 and 7, matching the pruned initialization from layers 7 and 8. Training performs k=4 forward passes on every k-th prefix; the substitution exists precisely so the first S−k memory vectors never have to be computed sequentially, which is what makes the Feedback Transformer affordable to train at all.

The paper also ranks its own components. Figure 5 orders them: sparse KV cache, attending to target activations, distillation loss, pruned initialization, feedback memory, MixedLoss. The supervision ranks second; the mixing mechanism fifth.

Rebuilt, trained, and measured

The mechanism was then implemented as specified — memory vectors replacing the keys and values, read strictly from previous positions, with the k in-flight positions using the draft’s own softmax-weighted mix over all its layer states and everything earlier substituted by the target’s activations — and the model retrained. Seven hours, 2.2× the wall time of the same step count without the recurrence, because training now needs k sequential forward passes per step.

It works, and it is the largest single component added here — but it delivers about two thirds of its published benefit. Three explanations were tested and all three are dead:

Measurement convention → ruled out

The ablation’s original 2.851 was taken under a fixed round count and the new figure under a token budget, and the stopping rule alone is worth ~3%. Re-running the ablation under the current harness gives 2.834 — 0.6%, inside run-to-run jitter.

Mixing weights stuck at initialization → ruled out

The weights are zero-initialized, which under a softmax is a uniform mix rather than a no-op, so the draft could plausibly have started somewhere unhelpful and stayed. It did not: the trained mix reads 0.264 / 0.634 / 0.102 and 0.115 / 0.555 / 0.330 against 0.333 uniform. Both layers concentrated on the first layer’s output.

A window off-by-one → ruled out

Feedback memory reads m<t strictly, so a window of 64 leaves 63 readable memory vectors where the paper may intend 64. Measured at both: SPIRe moves 3.127 → 3.131, 0.14 standard errors. MagicDec, which has no feedback memory and does read its own slot, moved seven times more — the opposite of the prediction, and both inside noise.

What remains is a training-data effect that cannot be fixed by retraining: the surviving mirror holds ~10% of the corpus, and at the paper’s document length it yields 0.65B tokens against a 2.37B budget, so this work uses documents four times longer and four times fewer. That fits the shape of the gap — the dense draft is 2% low and both sparse drafts are 4–8% low, and a draft learning to exploit a 64-token window plus target activations plausibly needs more document diversity than one that simply reads everything.

Cost modelmeasured

The paper's speedups derive from ITM = (speculate + verify) / target, the cost of one speculative round relative to one plain decode step, with end-to-end speedup τ / ITM. Measuring it directly requires controlling several confounds that are easy to miss; the harness holds the token budget fixed rather than the round count, pins the KV allocation identically across depths, measures actual tokens generated rather than assuming them, and cross-checks its own τ against the independent acceptance harness.

Every absolute lands at 0.75–0.76 of prediction. But the paper’s claims are ratios between methods, and a common factor cancels out of a ratio. It claims “over 100% versus vanilla, over 35% versus MagicDec”; measured on hardware at its own operating point, SPIRe delivers +104% over vanilla against a published +104%, and +33% over MagicDec against +36%. The cost model mispredicts every absolute throughput by a quarter and predicts the differences between methods almost exactly — which is what a roofline model omitting a constant overhead fraction should do, and it means the paper’s comparative conclusions survive measurement even though its absolute numbers do not.

Two things only measurement shows

Vanilla speculative decoding is a net loss in this regime. 1.021× at k=4 is barely break-even, and it falls below 1.0 from k=5 to reach 0.815× at k=8. The model predicts 1.36×. At B=64 and L=512 the dense draft’s KV reads cost more than its acceptance buys, and no analytical term in the model captures it.

Only the dense draft has a depth that matters. Vanilla peaks sharply at k=2 (1.091×) and degrades hard past it — 25% lost by k=8, and below break-even from k=5. Both sparse methods are essentially flat from k=4 onward: SPIRe varies 3.9% across k=4…8 and MagicDec 1.7%, against ~1% timing jitter. The useful statement is the asymmetry: deep speculation is safe for a sparse draft and actively harmful for a dense one. A dense draft re-reads the whole context on every one of its k passes, so each extra token of depth costs it the full cache; a windowed draft pays a constant. That is the cost-side counterpart of the acceptance finding in §4, and the one direction a single k=4 evaluation cannot show.

The sparse cache is now real, and building it corrected the model

Until recently the sparse drafts’ cache was an attention mask over a full-length allocation. Distributions and τ were exact — a masked entry contributes precisely zero — but the draft still read every position from memory, so the bandwidth saving that is SPIRe’s entire cost argument was not realized, and timing it would have measured the implementation rather than the method. It is now a compact ring: sink + window + k entries, verified to produce bitwise identical draft distributions to the masked version across every round and both RoPE conventions.

That + k is not padding, and it is a correction to the paper. A ring of sink + window — the size the cost model charges for — cannot run speculative decoding at all: the draft writes k tokens ahead speculatively, and when they are rejected the next query still needs entries those writes evicted. Measured both ways, the undersized ring diverges from the reference by 1.1e−01 while the correct one agrees to zero. At the paper’s window 64 and k=4 the cost model understates the draft’s cache by 6.2%.

A second finding fell out of transcribing the model. The appendix adds SPIRe one extra FLOPs term — projecting a memory vector into a key and a value — and at the paper’s own operating point that term changes nothing. The draft is memory-bound there by 8.4×, so the roofline maximum never sees it; deleting it entirely leaves every published throughput figure bit-identical. It only bites at batch 512 with contexts of a few tens of tokens, well outside the plotted region. It is worth noting for what it prices: that term is the cost of feedback memory, the component the paper’s own ablation ranks fifth of six at +0.049 τ. A component whose benefit is 1.5% and whose modelled cost is exactly zero.

Context length: the mechanism itselfmeasured

Everything above is measured at a single context length. But SPIRe’s entire argument is about what happens as context grows: the target’s KV cache grows with L while a windowed draft’s does not, so a sparse draft should get cheaper relative to the target the longer the context gets. That is the mechanism the method is named for, and it had never been put on a clock.

It needs no retraining. ITM is a ratio of timings over fixed shapes — it does not ask whether the model is any good at the length being timed, only what the hardware does — so it is measurable at context lengths the 67M target was never trained for.

The ceiling, and three wrong diagnoses

The ladder stops at 2560, and MagicDec at 1920. Reaching even that took three attempts, and the first two diagnoses were both wrong:

  • Blamed attention. The byte count said otherwise: 64 × 2561 × 50304 × 4 matches the reported 32,980,107,264 exactly. It was the unembedding — both prefill paths projected every prompt position to vocabulary and then read a single column. 33 GB materialized to keep 12.9 MB.
  • The fix never ran. The harness builds three generators — a calibration probe plus two timed runs — and the new flag reached only the timed pair. Byte-identical error messages should have said so at once.
  • Same bug, third site. The real fix reuses a flag added when this exact mistake OOMed feedback-memory training at 117 GB. A projection to vocabulary inside anything that runs over a whole sequence costs 200 KB per position per row: nothing at L=1, 33 GB at L=2560.

What still caps the ladder is the attention term in MagicDec’s dense draft prefill, which uses an explicit mask rather than FlashAttention (§9). Clearing it means FlashAttention or a smaller batch — and a smaller batch would confound B with L, which is the one thing this sweep exists to separate.

8

Correctionscorrected

Eight results reported during this work were later found wrong and revised. They are listed because the pattern connecting them is itself the most transferable finding here.

Cost model is 2–3× optimistic → roughly 1.3×

The first timing harness held the round count fixed across depths, so higher k committed more tokens per round and walked the cache further. Worse and less visible, the speculative cache is sized from a worst case that scales with k, so depth 8 was charged for roughly twice the buffer depth 1 was, even at matched token counts. Both are now controlled.

A fixed per-pass overhead explains the gap → it does not

Solving for one constant per forward pass gave values from 0.81 to 6.34 ms that scaled with batch size, and one cell produced a negative work term. The hypothesis was rejected on its own evidence rather than retained as a plausible story.

Feedback memory is worth only 0.049 τ → the bundle is worth 0.442

The paper's ablation table separates the feedback-memory mechanism from the target-activation substitution it carries. Reading the first number alone made the component look cheap to skip; it is 59% of SPIRe's advantage over vanilla.

Sparse drafts cannot be timed → they can, once the cache is compact

The blocker was real but it was an implementation gap, not a limit. A compact ring of sink + window + k now backs both sparse drafts, bitwise identical to the masked version. Building it turned up two things the mask had hidden: the cost model charges for a ring one speculation depth too small to run, and the rotary table was being sized by cache length rather than by position — correct by coincidence on a dense cache, and silently wrong on a compact one for exactly the long contexts the method exists to serve.

The timed cache was sized for every draft being accepted → sized from measured τ

The harness pinned its KV allocation using k+1 tokens per round — the maximum — where measured τ runs 1.8 to 4.1. Attention reads the whole allocation, so the surplus was charged to every cell, and not evenly: a dense draft reads all of it while a windowed draft reads its window regardless. The slack fell entirely on vanilla, which is the control. Corrected, the pin drops 14%.

The timing harness was measuring the compiler → jit hoisted out of the call

The speculative entry point read return jax.jit(fn)(…) inside its call body, building a fresh wrapper on every invocation. JAX keys its compilation cache on that wrapper, so each call missed and recompiled the whole program. A speculative call cost 7–22 seconds against plain decode’s 54 ms; that was compile time. After the fix a zero-round call costs 53–54 ms in all three modes against a 53.7 ms plain prefill, and per-round costs order as physics requires — SPIRe cheapest, vanilla dearest.

Three earlier fixes preceded this one and none was the cause: differencing two separate compilations, per-row rotary on a dense cache, and stacking per-round distributions no one reads. All three were real defects. None could have produced a sane number while the harness was timing its compiler. Each was diagnosed by reasoning about a differenced quantity with several faults compounded into it; what worked was timing the parts — prefill alone, then t(R) at R = 0, 4, 12, 38 — which took twenty minutes and was available from the first bad result.

k=4 is optimal for none of the three → only vanilla has a locatable optimum

Reported as “SPIRe peaks at k=5, MagicDec at k=7.” Those are the arithmetic maxima, but MagicDec’s throughput curve varies 1.7% across k=4…8 against ~1% timing jitter, and SPIRe’s 3.9%. Naming a peak on a curve that flat is reading a maximum off a straight line — the same error that produced the withdrawn “τ is not constant in L” claim in §4b, committed again on a different axis. Vanilla’s optimum is real: its curve varies 25.4%, and the cost model predicts its location correctly.

Every τ measured before the decode fix → invalid

Training feeds inputs shifted right by one with position zero masked to a sentinel; the decode path fed raw prompts. The model therefore answered "what token did I just see" rather than "what comes next", and greedy generation repeated its own last token indefinitely — while eval loss read a healthy 2.534.

Three separate times, an internal metric agreed with itself while the deployed behavior was broken: the decode off-by-one (eval loss healthy, generation degenerate), the memory leak (training loss better than a correct run), and the first feedback-memory draft (loss 0.75, τ 1.000). The original test suite could not have caught any of them. It compared cached against non-cached, draft against target, generation against its own reference — the implementation against itself, green under any consistent error.

The suite added since checks against things known independently, and every check is paired with a teeth test that fails if the check has become vacuous. A disabled feature must return the plain model bitwise. The memory projection must equal what a forward pass writes into the cache, bitwise. At a block size of one the mixing weights must be provably inert. The compact cache must match the masked one across every round. And the decode path must match a no-cache pass built from training semantics — which is what caught the rotary-table bug, since neither τ nor any loss curve can see it.

One detail of that last test is worth repeating. Comparing sampled tokens passed a cache that was genuinely broken: an untrained draft’s logits are near-uniform, so its argmax is decided by float noise. Comparing the distributions separates “different summation order” at 1e−07 from “different attention inputs” at 1e−01. The observable was the difference between a green suite and a real one.

9

What these numbers can and cannot be compared against

Measurement floor

Greedy self-drafting, where the draft is the target, must give τ = k+1 exactly. It measures 4.975–4.986. The draft evaluates one token at a time while verification evaluates k+1 in a batch; in bfloat16 those reduce in different orders and near-tied argmaxes disagree. The shortfall is a systematic downward bias on every τ reported here, it is the ceiling no draft can exceed, and the analytical model cannot see it. It is also not reproducible run to run — the framework disables deterministic GPU reductions — so differences below about 0.03 between runs mean nothing.

Statistical power

The paper measures τ over n = 65,536 contexts, reporting 95% intervals of ±0.004. Every figure in §2 comes from ~1,200 contexts under the paper’s own token budget, giving intervals near ±0.020 — five times wider than published, and tight enough to resolve the ~0.05 differences the comparison turns on. Earlier figures in this work came from 128 contexts × 8 rounds at ±0.10, wide enough to swallow several comparisons made at the time; those have all been retaken. A conservative per-context interval is also reported alongside each figure, since rounds inside one context share a prefix and are not independent draws.

Known deviations

  • The mirror holds roughly 10% of the source corpus, and its non-contiguous chunks are reindexed densely on download.
  • Sequence length per document is 4096 rather than 1024, because the mirror's document count cannot otherwise reach the token budget. Same token count, drawn from about four times fewer distinct documents.
  • The paper contradicts itself on whether the draft’s cache grows with context: §3.1 says the mask keeps it “constant with respect to the decoding sequence length”, while the appendix cost model uses sink + L/8, which is not. They agree only at L=512. This work follows the prose for training and acceptance, and the code for cost.
  • Acceptance is measured at the paper’s exact grid, {256, 512, 960}, on a 7,680-token dataset build divisible by all three. An earlier grid of {256, 512, 1024} with a window scaled as L/8 was wrong on both counts and is withdrawn.
  • Hardware is H100 PCIe, giving an operational intensity of 756 rather than the SXM value of 600. The constant cancels exactly wherever all three roofline terms are memory-bound, which covers every grid cell at L ≥ 128.
  • Batch size, learning rate and sampling temperature are unspecified by the paper and chosen here; the learning rate was selected from a probe that found the range flat to within 2%.

Open

  • Absolute throughput on an optimized stack. The 25% the model is optimistic by is measured on this implementation — unfused kernels, a 67M model, no paged attention. A production stack sits closer to roofline, so the defensible claim is “the model omits costs that are real at these scales,” not a number that transfers. What does transfer is that the omission is close to a constant factor, so the ratios hold.
  • Attribution inside the memory pathway. The 69% capture in §5 cannot be split between target-activation substitution and feedback memory without the intermediate arm the paper publishes at 3.352. That is one 3-hour run and it is the outstanding diagnostic.
  • Long context. Everything here is at L ≤ 960, bounded by the target’s 1024-token training length. A genuine long-context evaluation needs a target trained at 4096 or beyond — which is where the paper’s extrapolation to 128K contexts actually lives.
